## 1. Mobility: slope limits, speed, range per day, energy vs slope, obstacles

### Takeaway
The published limits cluster at three slope thresholds. **15°** is the nominal limit for VIPER and the conservative planning value for EVA and robotic studies. **20°** is the requirement for the Artemis LTV and the hard EVA limit. **25°** is the "not traversable" cutoff in accessibility studies (VIPER could manage 25–30° only "when necessary", with slip). Robotic drive speeds are 10–20 cm/s, but VIPER-style average progress is only about 1 cm/s because of stops. Crew walking is planned at about 2 km/h within about 2 km of the lander. The LTV requirement is ≥20 km per charge and 6–8 km per 24 h when driven remotely.

### Cited Findings
**VIPER (NASA Ames; robotic, solar/battery, Earth-teleoperated)**
- Can generally drive on slopes up to 15°, and can tackle 25° or 30° "when necessary", but slipping becomes more likely and slows the rover — [NASA Science, VIPER Lunar Operations](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/lunar-operations/)
- Design limits listed as "20 km max drive distance (design limit)", "15 deg slopes + 20 cm step obstacles (design limit)" and "10 cm/s drive speed while prospecting (instrument limit)" — [NASA NTRS, VIPER presentation 2025-04-24](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250004148/downloads/viper-2025-04-24.pdf)
- "Designed to navigate slopes up to a maximum of 15° to ensure its stability and safety" — [Beyer et al. 2025, VIPER Site Analysis, PSJ 6:236](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae061a)
- Drive: top speed 20 cm/s, nominal 10 cm/s — [Bluethmann, VIPER Rover Overview and Mobility, LSIC 2024 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240013903/downloads/LSIC_2024-Tech%20Infusion_Panel-VIPER_Mob-HW.pdf). Another NASA page gives a top speed of 0.45 mph (0.72 km/h, i.e. 20 cm/s) — [NASA NTRS VIPER overview/CATIE expo](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230011558/downloads/VIPER%20Mission%20Overview%20Presentation-%20CATIE%20Expo.pdf) (the search extract did not make clear which of the listed NASA pages held the mph figure)
- Planning assumption: "1 cm/s average speed · 10 cm/s when moving (waiting 90% of time)" — [An Overview of Mission Planning for the VIPER Rover, NASA 2022](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf)
- Total traverse goal about 20 km ("20+ km total drive") — [Colaprete, VIPER PIP (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210015009/downloads/20210015009%20-%20Colaprete-VIPER%20PIP%20final.pdf); [Fong, VIPER 2024-08-01 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240008614/downloads/viper-2024-08-01.pdf?attachment=true)
- About 1,700 m of planned driving inside PSRs (a 2025 NASA VIPER slide; the extract did not say which 2025 deck) — [VIPER Industry Day briefing, Feb 2025](https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/psd/lunar-science/documents/2025-02-13_VIPER_Updated_Technical_Briefing_Presentation-Shared.pdf) / [viper-2025-04-24](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250004148/downloads/viper-2025-04-24.pdf)

**Artemis Lunar Terrain Vehicle (LTV; crewed and remotely operated)**
- LTV-SYS-029: at least 20 km without recharge at full performance. LTV-SYS-071: 6 km threshold / 8 km goal in 24 hours under remote operation. Slope capability of 20° in the up, down and cross directions — [Litaker et al., NASA-TM-20240001217, LTV Remote Teleoperation Studies](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/artemis/resources/LitakerEtAl_NASA-TM-20240001217_LTV%20Teleops%20Study.pdf); also [IEEE version (NTRS 20240011392)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011392/downloads/Teleops%20IEEE%20Paper%20FY24%20FINAL.pdf?attachment=true)
- National Geographic reports about 6 miles (~10 km) range from the lander on one charge. This conflicts with the 20 km requirement and may be a walkback distance rather than battery range — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/premium/article/nasa-artemis-rover-lunar-terrain-vehicle-ltv)
- **A 15 km/h LTV top speed is NOT confirmed by a primary source.** It appears only on Grokipedia — [Grokipedia LTV](https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_Terrain_Vehicle). A Texas Tech student design based on the Apollo LRV used 4 m/s (14.4 km/h) top speed, 2.5 m/s cruise, 54 km range and 20° peak slope. - xEVA ConOps: unpressurized rover range of 10 km, derived from a ~5-hour walk back to the lander at 2 km/h — [NASA EVA-EXP-0042 Rev B xEVA System ConOps, 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/eva-exp-0042_xeva_system_con_ops_rev_b_final_dtd_10192020_ref_doc.pdf)

**Apollo LRV (analogue, equatorial)**
- Crews never drove beyond the 7.6 km "walkback limit", although the battery allowed 92 km. Ground clearance 0.3 m — [NASA OCHMO-TB-023 Extraterrestrial surface transport vehicles](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-023-extraterrestrial-surface-transport-vehicles-rovers.pdf?emrc=b45f92)

- EVA surface slope limit of less than 20° — [Artemis HLS document (NTRS 20220011114)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220011114/downloads/ArtemisHLSDDL_v3.pdf). A de Gerlache site study says "Any traverse needs to be on slopes less than 20 degrees" and adopts a more conservative planning threshold of <15° — [Artemis III 2023 workshop abstract #2009](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/Artemis_III_2023/pdf/2009.pdf)
- MoonPath (Peña-Asensio et al., Acta Astronautica, 2023–24) limits walking slopes to 15°, avoids mapped boulders and estimates travel time from Apollo 12/14 walking EVA data — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374733633_Automated_astronaut_traverses_with_minimum_metabolic_workload_Accessing_permanently_shadowed_regions_near_the_lunar_South_pole); [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2023-10-lunar-astronauts-easy-trails-moon.html)

**Energy vs slope / speed**
- Speyerer et al. 2016 "R-Traverse" couples a wheel–regolith (terramechanics) model with LRO data to find least-energy traverses — [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/optimized-traverse-planning-for-future-polar-prospectors-based-on/). A search extract attributes battery-sizing figures to this line of work: ~155 Wh at 500 m/h and ~190 Wh at 388.89 m/h (slower driving needs more battery because shadow is crossed more slowly). **The attribution is uncertain**: the figures may come from the paper below rather than Speyerer — [ResearchGate Speyerer 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299344300_Optimized_traverse_planning_for_future_polar_prospectors_based_on_lunar_topography)
- A 2026 Frontiers paper splits wheel–regolith resistance into compaction, bulldozing and gravitational (slope) terms. It runs a slope-penalized planner (600 traverses per target, 31 south-polar shadowed regions) and reports minimum round-trip mechanical energy starting at ~0.4 kWh, with median path lengths ~12–50 km — [Frontiers in Astronomy & Space Sciences, doi 10.3389/fspas.2026.1764826](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/astronomy-and-space-sciences/articles/10.3389/fspas.2026.1764826/full). A public repo that may be related (unverified link to the paper) is [github.com/amoeinn/lunar-polar-traverse](https://github.com/amoeinn/lunar-polar-traverse)
- Cannon & Britt 2020 computed minimum-energy, minimum-distance and lowest-max-slope routes into 39 south-polar cold traps >50 km². They assumed slopes >25° are not traversable — [Earth and Space Science, doi 10.1029/2020EA001291](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020EA001291)

**Obstacles / turning (analogues)**
- Yutu-2 (Chang'e-4, far side, **analogue**): gradeability 20°, obstacle height 200 mm (table citing a 2020 Yutu-2 paper) — [space-robotics.org Yutu-2](https://space-robotics.org/robots/lunar/yutu-2/). Its teleoperation listed curvature limits, obstacle-climbing ability and communication conditions as planning indicators — [ISPRS Annals 2020, Computer vision in the teleoperation of Yutu-2](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/V-3-2020/595/2020/isprs-annals-V-3-2020-595-2020.pdf)
- Pragyan (Chandrayaan-3, ~69.4°S, **analogue**): 26 kg, ~1 cm/s top speed — [Gunter's Space Page](https://space.skyrocket.de/doc_sdat/chandrayaan-3.htm). Drove 101.4 m in total — [Wikipedia Pragyan (Ch-3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragyan_(Chandrayaan-3))

### Inferences
- Speed assumptions differ by mode. At VIPER's planning average of 1 cm/s, a full 24 h gives about 0.86 km/day (own arithmetic). Continuous driving at 10 cm/s would give about 8.6 km/day, but VIPER never planned that. LTV-SYS-071 (6–8 km per 24 h, remote) is the best-sourced figure for an uncrewed LTV.
- A credible slope cost model for the app: **0–15° allowed (nominal)**, **15–20° allowed with a high cost penalty** (LTV/EVA hard limit 20°), **20–25° "exceptional only" for a VIPER-class rover** (more slip), and **>25° impassable**.
- The VIPER 20 cm step-obstacle limit and the Yutu-2 200 mm obstacle height are far below the resolution of LOLA terrain (5–20 m). Rock and step hazards therefore cannot come from the DEM alone (see Section 2).

### Gaps
- No primary NASA source was found for an LTV top speed (15 km/h is unverified), turning radius, or obstacle height. The LTV requirements document itself was not retrieved.
- No published Wh/km-vs-slope curve for VIPER or LTV was found. Only the terramechanics method (compaction, bulldozing, gravity terms) and order-of-magnitude round-trip energies are available.
- No VIPER turning-radius figure. VIPER has independently steered wheels, but no source was retrieved to confirm it here.

## 2. Hazards: rocks, boulders, craters, roughness, trafficability — which layers exist at the pole and at what resolution

### Takeaway
Most global hazard layers **stop short of the pole**: Diviner rock abundance covers ±70° and the 2025 NAC global boulder map covers ±60°. South-pole hazard data come from site-specific studies instead: NAC boulder and track mapping, the Boazman et al. inventories and VIPER site analysis. The best pole-wide topography is the LOLA 5 m south-pole DEM, which is ~94% interpolated with ~0.6 m vertical RMS. Meter-scale hazards (20 cm steps, 1–3 m boulders) need NAC DTMs or imagery, not LOLA.

### Cited Findings
- Diviner rock abundance mosaics are limited to ±70° latitude by Diviner rock-abundance coverage. Footprint is ~160 × 320 m from a 50 km polar orbit. The product best matches rocks larger than ~1–2 m, and values ≲0.35% are dominated by viewing geometry, composition and the noise floor — [Bandfield et al. 2011, JGR](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2011JE003866); [Powell et al. 2023, JGR Planets (high-res Diviner RA)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022JE007532) (the search extract did not separate which of these gave each number)
- Global NAC boulder map built with a CNN, **between 60°S and 60°N** (so it excludes the south pole). NAC images reach down to 0.5 m/px — [Aussel, Rüsch, Gundlach, Bickel et al. 2025, JGR Planets](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025JE008981); [EPSC 2024 abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024EPSC...17..998A/abstract)
- Rüsch & Bickel (2023) mapped ~130,000 fragmented boulders larger than ~10 m globally — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372437861_Global_Mapping_of_Fragmented_Rocks_on_the_Moon_with_a_Neural_Network_Implications_for_the_Failure_Mode_of_Rocks_on_Airless_Surfaces)
- Bickel & Kring 2020 (Icarus 348:113850) mapped 84 boulders with tracks poleward of 85°S, plus 13 tracks that enter Shackleton's PSR. They estimated regolith bearing capacity from track depth and local slope, mapped surface strength across the sunlit south-polar region, and found bearing capacity similar to equatorial regolith. Their example traverses cross maximum slopes of 10°, 15° and 16° — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Icar..34813850B/abstract); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103520302311)
- Boazman et al. 2023 (PSJ) mapped 86,896 isolated boulders, 3,556 rocky craters and 7,553 rock exposures around Artemis candidate sites 001 and 004. Targets on slopes <15° include the Shackleton rim and the "Connecting Ridge" — [Boazman 2022/23 PSJ PDF (Univ. Bern)](https://boris.unibe.ch/179288/1/Boazman_2022_Planet._Sci._J._3_275.pdf)
- At Mons Mouton (VIPER), a search extract reports no boulders ≥3 m in LROC images at 0.9 m/px, and about 40 rocks in Chandrayaan-2 images with median height 14 cm, under the ~20 cm drive-over limit. The extract did not say which document this came from; likely [Beyer et al. 2025 VIPER Site Analysis](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae061a) or [space-robotics.org VIPER page](https://space-robotics.org/robots/lunar/viper/). Mons Mouton is radar-dark, about 10% less reflective than typical terrain at S-band, which suggests a smoother surface or fewer large scatterers — [Beyer et al. 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae061a)
- LOLA 5 m/px south-pole DEMs (Barker et al. 2023, PSJ): RMS total offsets 8.7 / 9.5 m horizontal and 0.64 m vertical. In the gridded DTM, **94% of pixels are interpolated** — [Barker et al. 2023, A New View of the Lunar South Pole from LOLA](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1). The earlier 5 m LDEM had data in only ~10% of pixels near the pole and contained artifacts — [Barker et al. 2021, Improved LOLA elevation maps for south pole landing sites (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)
- LROC NAC vs LOLA 5 m grid: 56% of differences fall within the expected 2 m — [USGS Moon LRO South Pole DEM V&V report](https://astrogeology.usgs.gov/search/map/moon_lro_south_pole_dem_verification_and_validation_report). Meter-scale NAC DTMs of south-polar landing sites now exist, and the 5 m LOLA posting "cannot fully resolve meter-scale surface features" — [LROC NAC-derived Meter-scale Topography of the Moon's South Polar Landing Sites, PSJ 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae10a4)
- LOLA pulse-width roughness (within the ~5 m spot) exists, but the pulse-width roughness maps "suffered from erroneous values" and should be used with caution — [arXiv 2307.11616](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.11616)
- Yutu-2 (analogue): Navcam stereo at each waypoint produced a 0.02 m DEM, from which obstacle maps were made for path search. Hazcam/Navcam/Pancam DEMs were used to model slope, aspect, roughness and visibility — [Remote Sensing 2020, Vision-based decision support for Chang'e-4 path planning](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/4/624); [space-robotics.org Yutu-2](https://space-robotics.org/robots/lunar/yutu-2/)
- Pragyan (analogue): on 27 Aug 2023 it found a 4 m-wide crater about 3 m ahead, was commanded to retrace its path and took a new route. Metre-scale craters were found only by onboard cameras — [Tribune India (ISRO release)](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/india/chandrayaan-3-pragyan-rover-comes-across-big-crater-on-lunar-surface-retraces-path-isro-releases-fresh-pictures-539165)
- Bickel et al. (2021, Nature Communications) used deep learning (HORUS) to enhance NAC images inside PSRs, which is relevant to hazard mapping in shadow — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8460740/)

### Inferences
- For the app, a defensible layer stack is: LOLA slope (5 m or 20 m) as the main cost and mask. Add a sub-pixel roughness or "unresolved hazard" penalty, because LOLA cannot see the 20 cm steps that limit VIPER. Use optional per-site rock masks only where published (Boazman, Bickel & Kring, VIPER site analysis). Diviner rock abundance and the 2025 global boulder map **should not be presented as covering the pole**.
- The 94%-interpolated LOLA 5 m grid means slopes computed at 5 m inside track gaps are smoothed. The app should be honest that routes are "planning-grade, not drive-grade", and ideally show a confidence or data-density indicator.
- Trafficability (bearing capacity) can reasonably be treated as uniform. Bickel & Kring found polar sunlit regolith similar to equatorial, so slope and rocks dominate.

### Gaps
- No pole-wide crater-density or small-crater hazard layer with a stated resolution was found.
- ShadowCam resolution and coverage were not retrieved (only that it images PSRs).
- Exact Diviner RA coverage poleward of 70°S and the resolution of the Powell et al. 2023 product were not confirmed.
- Mini-RF CPR polar coverage and resolution were not retrieved.

## 3. Illumination and thermal: time-varying shadows, max time in shadow, PSR entry, thermal limits

### Takeaway
At the pole, shadows sweep across terrain **at about rover speed**. Planners therefore use time series of illumination and comms maps (VIPER: 2-hour steps, 4,128 maps), not static "% illumination". VIPER-class rovers must reach a sunlit "safe haven" before darkness longer than their hibernation survival (~50 h; older documents say 40–96 h). PSR excursions are battery-limited to about 6–10 h of working time. For crew, the xEVA concept allows up to 2 h of continuous shadow exposure.

### Cited Findings
- Sun shadows and radio shadows move at about the rover's average speed, so traverses must be timed to avoid moving shadows — [An Overview of Mission Planning for the VIPER Rover (NASA 2022)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf)
- VIPER mission design used time-series illumination and communications maps at a 2-hour step (4,128 maps) over the southern-summer landing season. The maps are 8-bit grayscale (0–255 = 0–100% illumination) — [Beyer et al. 2025, VIPER Site Analysis](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae061a)
- Safe havens are spots with sustained sunlight where shadow periods are under about 50 h. Traverses are planned so the rover returns to a safe haven for hibernation. Shadow at a safe haven may not exceed the hibernation survival time (~50 h) — [VIPER mission planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf); [Ennico-Smith et al., VIPER Mission Traverse Planning (SSR 2023, NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004239/downloads/SSR2023-VIPER-Planning-Ennico.pdf?attachment=true). The team also defined a safe haven as a location getting sunlight for a minimum duration while Earth is below the horizon
- Survival in shadow, by document date (conflicting): 50+ h hibernation in low-power mode — [VIPER Industry Day, Feb 2025](https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/psd/lunar-science/documents/2025-02-13_VIPER_Updated_Technical_Briefing_Presentation-Shared.pdf); 70 h design requirement in older material — [Colaprete PAC 2020](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/09_Colaprete_VIPEROverviewforPAC08172020.pdf); "Dark Survivability: 96hrs" and "Dark Hibernation: 40–80hrs (TBR)" — [Andrews, VIPER Mission Overview, Apr & Jun 2020 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003840/downloads/2020-06-24,%20VIPER%20Mission%20Overview%20(Andrews).pdf)
- Active work in shadow: ~9.5 h with drill — [VIPER mission planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf); batteries sized for 9 h of shadowed operations — [VIPER Industry Day 2025](https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/psd/lunar-science/documents/2025-02-13_VIPER_Updated_Technical_Briefing_Presentation-Shared.pdf); "PSR Working Duration: 6hrs" — [Andrews Apr 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205000864/downloads/2020-04-14,%20VIPER%20Mission%20Overview%20(Andrews).pdf); "PSR Working Duration (w/drill): 10hrs" — [Utz, The Rover Software of the VIPER Mission, 2021 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220000353/downloads/VIPER_RSW_GRAZ_V1_1.pdf)
- Thermal environment: ~40 K (−390°F) in PSRs to ~300 K in sun. One deck gives an expected cold environment of −230 °C — [Andrews 2020 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003840/downloads/2020-06-24,%20VIPER%20Mission%20Overview%20(Andrews).pdf)
- Nobile (the original VIPER site, chosen over Haworth and Shoemaker in traverse studies): darkness up to ~45 h listed under site-selection criteria (from a fragmentary slide) — [VIPER mission planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf)
- Speyerer et al. 2016 (Icarus 273:337–345) found a least-energy route on the Shackleton – de Gerlache connecting ridge that keeps a rover illuminated for 94.4% of the lunar year. Least-energy traverses were computed at a 20 m scale — [ASU Pure](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/optimized-traverse-planning-for-future-polar-prospectors-based-on/); [LROC feature "Planning the next steps"](https://www.lroc.asu.edu/images/937)
- Thermal-aware traverse planning reports 32–83% energy savings over the shortest traverse, depending on rover configuration and route — [Traverse Planning on the Lunar Surface – Benefits from Thermal Modeling (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/284030097_Traverse_Planning_on_the_Lunar_Surface_-_Benefits_from_Thermal_Modeling); related: [Lunar Traverse Planning with Integrated Thermal Simulation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343574662_Lunar_Traverse_Planning_with_Integrated_Thermal_Simulation) (the extract did not say which of the two gave the 32–83% figure)
- Research planners for solar rovers in shadowed regions combine offline mission-level path planning with an online "safe stochastic reachability" check, so the rover can always reach recharge or safety — [arXiv 2401.08558](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.08558); recovery policies: [arXiv 2307.16786](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.16786)
- Crew: xEVA ConOps allows up to 2 h of contiguous exposure in shadowed areas — [EVA-EXP-0042 Rev B](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/eva-exp-0042_xeva_system_con_ops_rev_b_final_dtd_10192020_ref_doc.pdf). NASA lighting guidance says south-pole surface operations "cannot assure completely lit surface operations" — [NASA Exploration Systems Exterior Lighting Design Guidance (NTRS 20240016463)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240016463/downloads/1-3-25%2020240016463.pdf?attachment=true). The Artemis III EVA tenets describe "intense light ... and intense dark (long shadows) due to the low sun angle" — [Tenets of Lunar EVAs for Artemis III (NTRS 20230008183)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230008183/downloads/Tenets%20of%20Lunar%20EVA%20for%20Artemis%20III_Public%20Release.pdf)
- Cold traps are permanently shadowed, which makes solar power impractical inside them. Cannon & Britt therefore favour nearby **staging areas** with sun visibility, Earth visibility and benign terrain — [Cannon & Britt 2020](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020EA001291)

### Inferences
- The app already computes sunlight over time, so a rover planner should use a **time-expanded graph**: (cell, time step) nodes, 1–2 h steps to match VIPER practice. An edge is allowed only if the rover's state of charge stays positive through any shadow it crosses. The route must end, or pause, at a "safe haven" cell whose longest upcoming shadow is shorter than the survival limit.
- Suggested defaults for a VIPER-class preset: max continuous shadow while active 6–9 h; safe-haven max shadow 50 h; PSR excursion ≤6 h. For an EVA preset: ≤2 h of continuous shadow.
- Because shadows move at about rover speed, a route that is sunlit "on average" can still fail. The app should show a shadow timeline along the route, not just mean illumination.

### Gaps
- No public numbers for LTV shadow survival, LTV thermal limits or suit cold-soak limits beyond the 2 h xEVA figure.
- Artemis III-specific PSR entry rules were not found in the retrieved extracts. An LPSC 2025 abstract on illumination constraints for Artemis III regions exists, but its contents were not retrieved — [LPSC 2025 #2655](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2025/pdf/2655.pdf)
- Speyerer 2016 rover speed and km/day assumptions were not retrieved.

## 4. Communications: DTE requirements, relay assumptions, max time without comms

### Takeaway
VIPER had **no relay**. It drove only with direct-to-Earth (X-band, DSN) line of sight and parked when Earth set. Radio shadows therefore act as moving no-go zones, just like solar shadows. NASA's LCRNS relay planning targets ~75% communications availability for surface systems. One relay brings worst and best south-pole sites to about 80–86% availability (DTE plus one relay), and two or three relays give 100%. ESA Moonlight is slated for initial service in 2028.

### Cited Findings
- VIPER used X-band DTE over the DSN with no relay; the short delay allowed interactive driving from Earth — [NASA NTRS VIPER overview](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230011558/downloads/VIPER%20Mission%20Overview%20Presentation-%20CATIE%20Expo.pdf); [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/viper-rover-could-help-long-term-human-exploration-of-moon/)
- VIPER is teleoperated, so it must keep DTE comms and avoid terrain radio shadows. When Earth drops below the horizon the rover must be parked — [Ennico, VIPER SRR June 2022 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220005327/downloads/VIPER-SRR-June2022-Ennico.pdf); radio shadows move at about rover speed — [VIPER mission planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf)
- VIPER's strategic planner SHERPA (AI-based activity and traverse planning) is paired with a tactical tool that overlays lighting, comms and terrain-hazard maps — [Fong, NASA Ames Lunar Rover Technologies 2024 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240010930/downloads/viper-tech-2024-08-26.pdf)
- LCRNS: one relay in a 6,500 km polar orbit sees the south pole 41% of the time, and three in one plane give 100%. With the relay, availability is 80–86% for worst/best landing sites. Target is ~75% communications availability for lunar-surface systems — [NASA LCRNS ESPER deck (2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/lcrns-esper-257-1.pdf)
- LCRNS PNT workshop: DTE plus one relay averages 80–86% yearly, and two relays reach 100%. The DTE-only values in the extracted slide text (17% / 43%) were garbled and should not be relied on — [IOAG Cislunar PNT workshop, LCRNS PNT services](https://ioag.org/Cislunar%20PNT%20Workshop/1a.%20Overview%20of%20Lunar%20PNT%20Frameworks%20&%20Systems%20(Part%20I)/03%20PNT%20Workshop%20LCRNS%20PNT%20Services_v6.pdf)
- The relay's main benefit is full south-pole coverage and better availability at sites without DTE visibility — [SpaceOps-2023 paper #537](https://publications.spaceops.org/2023/download_by_id.php?id=0537). Exact coverage requirements are in NASA's Lunar Relay Services Requirements Document (not retrieved) — [NASA Lunar Relay SRD](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/lunar-relay-services-requirements-document-srd.pdf?emrc=6914e527aa51a)
- ESA Moonlight: initial service planned 2028, full service 2030 — [ESA via UNOOSA ICG 2024](https://www.unoosa.org/documents/pdf/icg/2024/WG-B_Lunar_PNT_Jun24/LunarPNT_Jun24_01_04.pdf). Lunar Pathfinder relay slated for 2026 — [Wikipedia CLMSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Mission_Support_Services)
- Cannon & Britt 2020 chose PSR staging areas partly for Earth visibility (communications) — [Cannon & Britt 2020](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020EA001291)
- Yutu-2 (far-side analogue, Queqiao relay) listed "communication conditions" as a planning indicator — [ISPRS Annals 2020](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/V-3-2020/595/2020/isprs-annals-V-3-2020-595-2020.pdf)
- Lander line of sight: astronauts should stay within 2 km of the lander and ideally keep it in sight — [Phys.org summary of MoonPath](https://phys.org/news/2023-10-lunar-astronauts-easy-trails-moon.html)

### Inferences
- The app already computes Earth line of sight, so a "DTE-teleoperated (VIPER-like)" preset can make drive edges valid only when Earth is above the local horizon (with a terrain mask) at that time step. Parking or hibernation is allowed during Earth-set if the site is also a safe haven.
- A "relay-assumed" preset can relax this. The defensible way is to report the % of the route's time with DTE and let the user toggle a relay assumption, rather than invent relay coverage.

### Gaps
- No explicit "maximum time without comms" requirement was found for VIPER, the LTV or EVA. VIPER's constraint is implicit (drive only with DTE; hibernate through Earth-set). The Artemis surface comm availability requirement as a DTE % was not found in a primary requirements document; the ~75% target comes from an LCRNS deck.
- No minimum Earth elevation angle (e.g. degrees above the horizon for a DSN link margin) was retrieved.

## 5. How VIPER mission planners designed traverses (tools, legs, stations, Nobile/Mons Mouton plans, lessons)

### Takeaway
VIPER planning was hierarchical: strategic routes (AI-assisted, SHERPA) broken into **legs of one lunar day** that start at the landing site or a safe haven and pass through **Science Stations**, then refined by tactical planning on map overlays. Planning ran on 2-hour illumination and comm time series. The rover moved only about 10% of the time (1 cm/s average). No tool called "MAPP" was found in any retrieved source.

### Cited Findings
- Ennico-Smith et al., "VIPER Mission Traverse Planning – Design, Strategies, and Dynamics" (Space Science Reviews 2023, by NTRS filename): traverse built from legs, Science Stations as areas of interest, strategic planning with automated route planning followed by tactical planning, plans reviewed by the science team before launch — [NTRS 20230004239](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004239/downloads/SSR2023-VIPER-Planning-Ennico.pdf?attachment=true)
- A leg is one lunar day and starts at the landing site or a safe haven — [VIPER mission planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/atoms/files/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf)
- Candidate traverse study sites were Nobile, Haworth and Shoemaker, and Nobile was chosen. The planning overview lists darkness up to ~45 h there — [VIPER mission planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf). The target later became **Mons Mouton** (near Nobile), with a mission area of roughly 5 × 4 km — [Beyer et al. 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae061a)
- Prospectivity model for Mons Mouton combines ice-stability regions, max temperature, PSRs, slope, curvature, aspect, distance to PSRs and PSR density. Because of the extreme latitude, shadows fall on southwest-facing surfaces, especially the northeastern sides of craters — [Coyan et al. 2025, PSJ 6:105](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/adbc6c)
- SHERPA is the AI-based strategic planner; the tactical tool overlays lighting, comms and hazards — [Fong 2024 (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240010930/downloads/viper-tech-2024-08-26.pdf)
- Status: terminated July 2024 — [Wikipedia VIPER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VIPER_(rover)). On 19 Sept 2025 NASA announced that Blue Origin's Blue Moon MK1 would deliver VIPER to Mons Mouton (CS-7) — same source. A LEAG 2025 talk says 2027 candidate traverses are being evaluated in the same Mons Mouton mission area, with start months as early as Aug 2027. The slide dating is inconsistent, so treat this as unconfirmed — [Colaprete, LEAG 2025](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/leag2025/presentations/Wednesday/1530_Colaprete.pdf)
- The relevance of VIPER planning to Artemis is discussed in a 2026 PSJ paper (content not retrieved) — [PSJ ae4232](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae4232); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/405066859_The_Relevance_of_the_VIPER_Mission_to_NASA's_Artemis_Human_Exploration_of_the_Moon)

### Inferences
- The app can mirror VIPER's structure in simple form: (1) the user picks start and goal; (2) the planner finds candidate "stations" or safe havens (cells with short maximum shadow and good Earth visibility) between them; (3) it chains legs of ≤1 lunar day that end at a safe haven; (4) it scores each leg on time-dependent sun and Earth visibility. That maps directly onto layers the app already has.
- "Lessons" visible in the sources: (a) average speed is about 10× below drive speed; (b) moving shadows make timing as important as geometry; (c) pre-launch plans are reviewed by scientists, so routes should be explainable (show why each segment was chosen).

### Gaps
- **"MAPP" could not be confirmed** as a VIPER tool name in any retrieved source; the planners found were SHERPA plus a tactical tool. The name may be internal, or may belong to another mission.
- The detailed published Nobile traverse (station list, leg distances) and the 2024 Mons Mouton traverse could not be read because nasa.gov and iopscience fetches were blocked.
- No post-cancellation "lessons learned" document was found.

## 6. Artemis III EVA traverse studies, published south-pole traverse studies, and analogues

### Takeaway
Artemis walking EVAs are planned at about 2 km/h within about **0.5–2 km of the lander**. The bound comes from a ~1 h emergency walk-back, and one site analysis gives a max walking range of 1.38 km. Slopes must be **<20°**, with **<15°** as the common planning value. Published south-pole route studies (Speyerer 2016, Cannon & Britt 2020, Lemelin et al. 2021, Bickel & Kring 2020, Boazman et al. 2023, Peña-Asensio et al. MoonPath) all use LOLA-based slope (5–20 m) plus illumination and, in some cases, Earth visibility and boulder masks. These are the closest precedents for the app.

### Cited Findings
- Tenets of Lunar EVAs for Artemis III: walking range may be constrained to 500 m–2 km radially by suit consumables. The ~2 km maximum comes from a suit contingency assumption of ~1 h emergency return, which depends on lighting, terrain, O2, vehicle ingress and walk-back rate — [NTRS 20230008183](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230008183/downloads/Tenets%20of%20Lunar%20EVA%20for%20Artemis%20III_Public%20Release.pdf)
- A NASA presentation cites a max walking range of 1.38 km for one south-pole landing location — [Scoville final presentation (NTRS 20210026296)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210026296/downloads/Scoville%20Final%20presentation%20-%20compressed.pptx.pdf)
- de Gerlache Artemis III site study: terrain slope is the main challenge; traverses must be on slopes <20°, planned to <15° — [Artemis III workshop 2023 #2009](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/Artemis_III_2023/pdf/2009.pdf)
- MoonPath (Peña-Asensio et al.): 5 m/px LOLA, ≤15° slopes, boulder avoidance, 2 km radius, Monte Carlo plus Dijkstra minimum-cost paths. 94% of PSRs are accessible from crater rims, and one site gives access to 11 PSRs at higher workload — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374733633_Automated_astronaut_traverses_with_minimum_metabolic_workload_Accessing_permanently_shadowed_regions_near_the_lunar_South_pole); [CSIC repository](https://digital.csic.es/handle/10261/356943); [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/lunar-astronauts-will-need-easy-walking-trails-around-the-moons-south-pole). A follow-on NASA ICES-2025 traverse model maps spatially distributed metabolic rate — [ICES-2025-129 (NTRS 20250001829)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250001829/downloads/ICES%202025%20TraverseModel.pdf?attachment=true)
- Speyerer et al. 2016: R-Traverse least-energy routes; 94.4% lunar-year illumination on the Shackleton – de Gerlache ridge — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/optimized-traverse-planning-for-future-polar-prospectors-based-on/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299344300_Optimized_traverse_planning_for_future_polar_prospectors_based_on_lunar_topography)
- Cannon & Britt 2020: 55 of 59 large cold traps (93%) have at least one entry/egress path with max slope <25°. The average max slope on lowest-energy routes is 21°. South-pole one-way routes from staging areas are ~14–26 km (e.g. SP_UN15: 15.8 km, max slope 23°) — [Earth and Space Science 2020](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020EA001291)
- Lemelin et al. 2021 (PSJ 2:103): a ~20 km traverse from site A (88.52°S, 49.12°W) and a longer 92,254 m traverse, using Mazarico illumination results. The water-ice-bearing PSR database is on Zenodo — [PSJ abf3c5](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/abf3c5/meta); [Zenodo 4646092](https://zenodo.org/records/4646092)
- Bickel & Kring 2020: boulder tracks give bearing capacity; example traverses cross max slopes of 10°, 15° and 16° — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Icar..34813850B/abstract)
- Boazman et al. 2023: boulder, rocky-crater and outcrop inventories plus <15° slope access around Artemis sites 001/004 — [PDF](https://boris.unibe.ch/179288/1/Boazman_2022_Planet._Sci._J._3_275.pdf)
- Analogue, Yutu-2 (CNSA, far side): ground-based route selection with onboard local planning and emergency avoidance; 0.02 m Navcam DEMs per waypoint; high wheel-skid risk noted on a steep approach to a fin-shaped rock — [space-robotics.org](https://space-robotics.org/robots/lunar/yutu-2/); [MDPI Remote Sensing 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/4/624); [ResearchGate fin-shaped rock](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369458253_Technology_enabled_science_investigation_of_a_fin-shaped_rock_by_the_Yutu-2_rover_on_the_lunar_farside)
- Analogue, Pragyan (ISRO, ~69°S): ~1 cm/s, 101.4 m over about 12 days; retraced its path to avoid a 4 m crater found 3 m ahead; had not woken by 28 Sept 2023 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragyan_(Chandrayaan-3)); [Tribune India](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/india/chandrayaan-3-pragyan-rover-comes-across-big-crater-on-lunar-surface-retraces-path-isro-releases-fresh-pictures-539165)
- ESA: no ESA south-pole traverse study with numbers was found in this pass. The only ESA item retrieved is Moonlight comms timing (Section 4)

### Inferences
**Consolidated default constraint set for a "Lunar Site Planner" route tool** (each value traces to a citation above):

| Preset | Max slope (hard) | Preferred slope | Speed / progress | Range / radius | Shadow limits | Comms |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| VIPER-class robotic | 15° nominal (25° "when necessary") | ≤15° | 10 cm/s moving; ~1 cm/s average | ≤20 km total | ≤~9 h active shadow; safe haven shadow <50 h; PSR ≤6–10 h | drive only with DTE (Earth above horizon) |
| LTV (remote) | 20° up/down/cross | ≤15° | 6–8 km per 24 h | ≥20 km per charge | not published | relay or DTE (assumption) |
| LTV (crewed) | 20° | ≤15° | top speed unconfirmed (~10–15 km/h claims) | walkback-limited (~10 km per xEVA ConOps; Apollo used 7.6 km) | per EVA limits | crew voice; assume DTE/relay |
| Walking EVA | 20° | ≤15° | ~2 km/h | 0.5–2 km from lander (~1 h return) | ≤2 h contiguous shadow | keep lander in sight if possible |
| Accessibility study (Cannon & Britt) | 25° | — | — | 14–26 km one-way | staging areas need sun and Earth visibility | — |

- The app's existing 20 m LOLA terrain matches the scale of Speyerer 2016 (20 m) and is coarser than MoonPath (5 m). The app should state its resolution, and flag that slopes measured at 20 m understate local maxima.
- The planning approach closest to published practice is Dijkstra or A* on a slope-penalized cost, with hard masks (slope above the limit, known boulders, no-DTE when moving for teleoperated rovers), plus time dependence for sun and Earth. Robustness can be reported via Monte Carlo over DEM error (MoonPath).

### Gaps
- A formal Artemis III EVA traverse requirements document with final numbers (e.g. max EVA metabolic budget, PSR entry duration for Artemis III) was not retrieved; the figures are 2020–2023 concept values.
- ESA south-pole traverse studies and CNSA Chang'e-7 (south-pole rover) planning constraints were not found in this pass.
- Speyerer 2016's assumed speed, energy-per-meter and day-length values, and Lemelin 2021's slope and Earth-visibility criteria, need the full text. The available extracts did not include them.
