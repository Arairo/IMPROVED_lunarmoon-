# Upgrade the mid-field terrain, then route rovers

The biggest remaining accuracy gain for Lunar Site Planner does not need a new horizon algorithm. It needs better terrain between about 3 and 50 km from each site. That band still comes from the app's own 240 m grid, built from a thinned, averaged subset of LOLA laser shots. Replace that band, and the 1 km band, with NASA PGDA's orbit-adjusted LOLA grids: 20 m out to roughly 30–50 km, then 60–120 m beyond. Downsample in a way that keeps ridge crests. Simple geometry shows that the 8-point ridge gap corresponds to only about **0.3–0.4° of systematic horizon error**, which is the size of error that smoothed crests 3–30 km away produce. A realistic target against PGDA's 60 m maps is a median gap of about 1 point and a worst case of **3–5 points**. Going lower is not meaningful: at the same three ridge sites, **PGDA's own 60 m and 120 m maps differ by 12–20 points**, and published models of the Shackleton rim range from about 85% to 95%. Part of today's 8 points also comes from mismatched conventions: the app counts the Sun's centre during 2027 from a 2 m mast, while PGDA reports the visible fraction of the Sun's disk averaged over 18.6 years. Separating that out takes hours, not weeks. A rover route planner is feasible in the browser as a strategic planning aid. In benchmarks run for this research, a 6 km route on a 20 m grid took **15–52 ms** and a time-aware search with waiting took **13–31 ms**. Computing horizons for every cell along the route took **3–10 s** on one thread, so that step belongs in Web Workers. The limits are serious and must be stated plainly. The 20 m LOLA terrain is mostly interpolated between laser tracks. It cannot show the 20 cm steps or the metre-scale boulders and craters that stop real rovers, and no rock map covers the pole. Every proposed route is therefore a sketch for discussion, not a path anyone could drive.

## Four kinds of evidence, and most source PDFs could not be opened

This report rests on four research notes written on 25 November 2026. During that session the network policy blocked pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov, ntrs.nasa.gov, iopscience.iop.org, hou.usra.edu, nasa.gov, lpi.usra.edu, arxiv.org and mdpi.com, so **almost no primary paper was read in full**. The literature findings come from abstracts, product pages and search-engine extracts. Headline numbers (resolutions, percentages, slope limits) are reasonably reliable. Method details are the weakest part: exact horizon distance limits, azimuth steps, observer heights and time steps behind PGDA's maps could not be confirmed. Some extracts conflict. For example, one source says Mazarico et al. 2011 used 1-hour steps and another says 6-hour steps. **Re-check PGDA file names, extents and sizes against the live listings** before building on them. The project's GitHub Actions runner can reach PGDA (`tools/pgda_fetch.py`).

To keep sources apart, the report tags its evidence. A plain inline link is a **cited finding**. *[Team calculation]* marks geometry worked out by the research team (Moon radius 1737.4 km), with no paper behind it. *[Team benchmark]* marks micro-benchmarks run single-threaded in Node 22, which uses the same V8 engine as Chrome, on a 4-core machine. These used **synthetic fractal terrain and synthetic shadow masks, not real LOLA tiles, and were not run on phones**. *[App record]* marks the app's own validation outputs in the repository. A few statements come from reading the app's build scripts while writing this report, and are marked as such.

## The 8-point ridge gap sits where the references themselves disagree

The app's site validation compares its sunlight share with PGDA's finest map at each of 22 south-polar sites. The median gap is **1.2 points** and the worst is **8.1** *[app record, `docs/validation/pgda.json`]*. The three worst sites are all ridge tops, and at all three the app reads **sunnier** than PGDA's 60 m map:

| Site | App, 20 m near field | App, 240 m only | PGDA 60 m | PGDA 120 m |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shackleton connecting ridge | 63.6% | 74.1% | 55.5% | 67.7% |
| Peak near Shackleton | 63.6% | 77.1% | 55.6% | 72.3% |
| Best-lit #1 | 66.4% | 66.4% | 59.4% | 79.7% |
| Malapert Massif (Artemis III) | 21.6% | 42.5% | 27.9% | 38.1% |

Two features of this table matter more than the 8-point headline. First, **the error has both signs**: at Malapert Massif the app is 6.3 points *darker* than PGDA, so a single global bias cannot explain the gaps. Second, **PGDA's 60 m and 120 m maps differ by 12–20 points at exactly these ridges**. PGDA's number is therefore a model output that is itself highly sensitive to resolution, not ground truth.

The literature shows the same spread. At the Shackleton rim, Kaguya topography gave about 86% ([Bussey et al. 2010](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010Icar..208..558B)) and Mazarico's 240 m LOLA model 85.5% ([Mazarico et al. 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251730356_Illumination_conditions_of_the_lunar_polar_regions_using_LOLA_topography)). A year of LRO wide-angle camera images found spots lit about 94% of the time ([Speyerer & Robinson 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256719684_Persistently_illuminated_regions_at_the_lunar_poles_Ideal_sites_for_future_exploration)), and a 20 m model of the connecting ridge gave **92.27% at 2 m height and 95.65% at 10 m** ([Gläser et al. 2014](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Icar..243...78G)). A 2026 A&A paper notes that the 240 m Mazarico model "tends to overestimate illumination at some locations due to the low resolution and smoothening of the topography" ([A&A 2026](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2026/10/aa61899-26/aa61899-26.html)). PGDA's 60 m illumination maps descend from that 2011 work ([PGDA product 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)). One abstract describes the original elevation models as about 240 m ([Gläser et al. 2014 abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103514004278)). What terrain the 2016 map update used is unknown, but it predates the 2023 orbit-adjusted grids.

Geometry shows how little horizon error 8 points takes. At the pole the Sun stays within about ±1.5° of the horizontal over the year. Sunlight share therefore changes by roughly **21% per degree of horizon bias, or about 2 points per 0.1°**, so an 8-point gap means the skyline is wrong by about 0.3–0.4° over the azimuths where the Sun rises and sets *[team calculation]*. A height error δh at distance d moves the horizon by about δh/d:

| Distance | Horizon shift from a 20 m height error | Height error allowed for a 0.05° shift |
|---|---|---|
| 3 km | 0.38° | 2.6 m |
| 10 km | 0.11° | 8.7 m |
| 30 km | 0.04° | 26 m |
| 100 km | 0.01° | 87 m |

*[Team calculation]*

A 240 m grid easily lowers narrow crests and rims by tens of metres, which gives 0.1–1° of bias in the 3–30 km band but only about 0.03° at 100 km. Distance still matters on ridges, though. An observer standing 1 km above the surrounding terrain looks down to a horizon about 59 km away, at about −1.9° elevation. So the sunrise and sunset azimuths on high ridges are set by terrain tens of kilometres to about 150 km away *[team calculation]*. This fits NASA's early finding that south-pole illumination depends mainly on distant high ground in the Malapert region, about 120 km from the pole ([Fincannon 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070034951/downloads/20070034951.pdf); [JPL IPN PR 42-176](https://ipnpr.jpl.nasa.gov/progress_report/42-176/176C.pdf)).

Reading the app's build scripts while writing this report showed why its mid-field is smooth. The 240 m and 1 km polar grids come from `tools/build_polar_dem.py`. It reads LOLA shots from the USGS point-cloud archive, using only the upper octree levels ("a few hundred MB, not 4 GB"), so only a thinned subset of the shots. It averages the shots that fall in each cell, fills gaps between tracks from the nearest neighbour, and applies a 3×3 mean to soften seams. The global grid is block-averaged to about 3.8 km (`tools/build_global_dem.mjs`). **Every one of these steps lowers narrow crests**, which is the documented direction of error. None of it uses the track-adjusted LOLA solutions, whose adjustment reduced orbit geolocation errors by more than a factor of 10 ([Barker et al. 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0032063320303329)).

The app's own records point the same way. On the connecting ridge, switching the first 3 km from 240 m to 20 m terrain cut sunlight from 74.1% to 63.6%. At Best-lit #1 that switch changed nothing (66.4% either way), yet the app still reads 7 points above PGDA. That leaves either terrain beyond 3 km or the conventions as the cause. The independent check against an LROC photo of the Peak near Cabeus B region agrees in **93.5% of cells (κ 0.87)** with 20 m terrain. Almost every miss is a cell the app calls lit that the photo shows dark, and the opposite never happens *[app record, `docs/VALIDATION.md`]*. The app's shadows run slightly short, which is what smoothed terrain would produce.

Conventions explain another part of the gap. The headline site comparison counts the **Sun's centre in 2027 from a 2 m mast**. PGDA reports the **visible fraction of the Sun's disk averaged over a multi-year cycle**. The app's notes describe PGDA's numbers as surface values, but the literature search could not confirm PGDA's observer height. Height matters a lot on narrow crests. Gläser's +3.4 points from 2 m to 10 m works out to roughly **0.4 points per metre**. Lowering an observer by 10 m raises the horizon 1 km away by 0.57° *[team calculation from Gläser 2014]*. A 60 m pixel's elevation can also sit 5–20 m below the crest point of a 20 m grid. The app already has a like-for-like check that matches PGDA's disk fraction and 18.6-year cycle (`tools/pgda_like_for_like.mjs`), but that check keeps the 2 m mast and the app's own site coordinates.

What accuracy is realistic? Sampling is no longer the problem. The app's own study puts skyline sampling error at **0.2 points of sunlight and 0.1 points of Earth visibility**, and ephemeris error at 0.007° *[app record]*. Terrain and conventions are what remain. Against PGDA's 60 m maps, a realistic target is a **median of about 1 point and a worst case of 3–5 points at ridges**, which is about the spread between independent high-quality methods *[team inference from the published spread]*. Against the real Moon, the limit is LOLA itself: crests that fall between laser tracks are interpolated low.

Earth visibility is much less sensitive. Earth's elevation swings about ±6.7° over a month, against about ±1.5° for the Sun, so the same horizon bias costs about **a quarter as much** in Earth visibility (about 4.8% per degree against 21% per degree) *[team calculation, using standard libration values]*. The like-for-like check already agrees within about 1 point at most 20 m sites, for example Haworth 44.1% against 43.9% and IM-2 93.9% against 93.6% *[app record]*.

## PGDA's adjusted grids can replace every outer band

### Data products worth using, and those to skip

The 2023 PGDA product 90 family is the current reference for the south pole. Its 20 m grid is Barker et al.'s "reference topographic product" ([Barker et al. 2023 preprint](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf)). All files are cloud-optimized GeoTIFFs in south polar stereographic metres, and they come with per-pixel height-error and effective-resolution rasters ([PGDA product 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90)). Kilometre half-widths below are computed from the polar stereographic formula *[team calculation]*.

| Product | Pixel | Coverage | Size | Role in the app | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LDEM_80S_20MPP_ADJ (+ _ERR, _EFFRES) | 20 m | 80–90°S, about ±304 km | about 2.6 GB | Near and mid field out to 30–50 km; source for a crest-preserving 60 m band | [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90) |
| LDEM_83S_10MPP_ADJ | 10 m | to about 83°S (exact extent unclear) | about 4.8 GB | Optional near-field upgrade | [Barker 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf) |
| LDEM_60S_60/120/240MPP_ADJ | 60–240 m | 60–90°S, ±931 km | 240 m file about 210 MB | Replace the 1 km grid; fill horizons for sites near 80°S | [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90) |
| PGDA 81 mosaic + count map | 5 m | 87–90°S, about ±91 km | about 3.3 GB | First few km near the pole; measure which pixels have real shots | [PGDA 81](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/81) |
| PGDA 104 shape-from-shading | 5 m | 13 Artemis III candidate regions, more than 4,500 km² | 26.6 GB on Zenodo | Later: first few km at Artemis sites | [Zenodo 17954508](https://zenodo.org/records/17954508) |
| LROC NAC stereo DTMs | 1–4 m | A few sites | varies | Route slopes where available | [PSJ 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae10a4) |
| SLDEM2015; Chang'e-2 CE2TMap2015 | 59 m; 20 m | stops at 60°S; LOLA offset 43 m mean (SD 110 m) | — | **Skip** | [PGDA 54](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/54); [Remote Sensing 12:526](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/3/526) |

A "5 m" or "20 m" label does not mean that much measured detail. About **90% of pixels in the 5 m polar grids are interpolated** because of LOLA's track spacing ([PGDA product 78](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/78)); another extract gives 94% for the 2023 grids ([Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1)). Inside the 5 m site fields, Barker et al. 2021 report RMS height errors of **0.30–0.50 m** and slope errors of 1.5–2.5° ([Barker et al. 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0032063320303329)). For horizons at kilometre distances, a height error of half a metre does not matter. Missing crests do. Effective resolution gets worse with distance from the pole; a search extract says that most of the Artemis zone south of 84°S is close to 20 m effective ([Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1/meta)). The newer products are regional patches, not pole-wide grids: shape-from-shading DEMs over the Artemis regions ([PGDA 104](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/104)) and a deep-learning 20 m reconstruction with 5–6 m test error ([PGDA 106](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/106)). A third-party review warns that these are "enhanced regional reconstructions, not continuous blanket 5 m terrain" ([cosmolabe #389](https://github.com/AaronPlave/cosmolabe/issues/389)).

### Keep the horizon method, change the distance bands

The app's architecture already matches the published one. Each study precomputes a 360° horizon for each point and then tests the Sun or Earth against it over an 18.6-year cycle. The studies differ mainly in DEM resolution: 240 m for [Mazarico 2011](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120010094), 20 m with a local 2 m patch for [Gläser 2014](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014Icar..243...78G), and 5 m for Barker 2021. Newer work adds a Cartesian topographic pyramid, with fine, small layers near the point and coarse, wide layers farther out ([Tong et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019103522003943)). It also adds 0.5° spoke tracing with curvature included ([A&A 2026](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2026/10/aa61899-26/aa61899-26.html)). The terrestrial code HORAYZON warns that **simplifying remote terrain can break a 0.25° accuracy requirement**, because rays miss thin features ([HORAYZON, GMD 2022](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/15/6817/2022/)). The two research notes proposed slightly different bands; reconciled, they give:

| Distance from site | Today | Proposed | From |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 km | 20 m | 20 m (5 m optional at Artemis sites) | LDEM_80S_20MPP_ADJ |
| 3 to about 30–50 km | 240 m | 20 m | LDEM_80S_20MPP_ADJ |
| about 30–300 km | 240 m to 180 km, then 1 km | 60 m, crest-preserving | 20 m resampled, or LDEM_60S_60MPP_ADJ |
| about 300–900 km | 1 km to 460 km, then 3.8 km | 120–240 m | LDEM_60S_120/240MPP_ADJ |

The cost is modest because all of this runs offline. Per azimuth, today's bands cost about 150 samples (20 m to 3 km) plus about 740 (240 m to 180 km). Extending 20 m to 15 km adds about 600 samples, and adding a 60 m band from 15 to 50 km adds about 580 *[team calculation]*. Instead of guessing the cut-off, measure it. Recompute Connecting Ridge and Peak near Shackleton with 20 m terrain out to 10, 20 and 40 km, and stop where the sunlight share moves by less than 0.5 point.

Two details decide whether this works. The first is downsampling. Mean or bilinear resampling lowers summits, which over-predicts sunlight. Taking the maximum over-raises the horizon instead, in the same way Barker found that height noise is "more likely to raise than to lower the horizon" ([Barker et al. 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009660/downloads/Mike%20Barker_%20Paper_%20Improved%20Lola.pdf)). Build both a mean pyramid and a max pyramid and report the answer as a range between them. The second is ray steps. The app's rays grow to 1.5% of the distance, which is about 450 m at 30 km, or about 22 cells of 20 m terrain. Its crest refinement re-samples only around the highest sample on each ray. The 0.2-point sampling bound was measured on today's smooth grids, so **re-run the sampling study after extending 20 m terrain outward** *[observation from reading `src/core/horizon.js` and `docs/VALIDATION.md`]*.

### Earth visibility depends on one nearside wedge

NASA's LOLA products count Earth as visible when **any fraction of its disk** is above the terrain ([Lunar Surface Data Book](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230007818/downloads/ACD-50044%20Lunar%20Surface%20Data%20Book%20Rev%20A.pdf)). Counting the disk's centre instead shifts the threshold by about 0.95°, which is worth **4–5 points** *[team calculation]*. Most Artemis III regions see Earth about 50% of the time, and the nearside half of Mons Malapert about 100% ([Mazarico & Barker 2023](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/Artemis_III_2023/pdf/2007.pdf)). Terrain cuts Earth visibility by up to 80% compared with a smooth Moon in crater-rim regions ([IEEE GRSL 2025](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025IGRSL..22L1520W/abstract)). Earth moves only about ±8° in azimuth around the sub-Earth direction, so only that wedge needs fine terrain. At many sites the wedge contains the Malapert massif, about 120 km away, so the mid-to-far field matters more there than the first 3 km *[team calculation]*. These products give geometric line of sight only, not a radio link budget ([LunarRF preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34554)).

### Pitfalls that quietly bias horizons

Most of these errors produce no error message, only wrong numbers.

**File formats and units.** PDS LOLA images are headerless 16-bit integers. Height is DN × 0.5 m; adding the 1,737,400 m offset gives the radius, not the height ([PDS LOLA FAQ](https://pds-geosciences.wustl.edu/missions/lro/lola_faq.htm); [ODE GDRDEM help](https://ode.rsl.wustl.edu/Moon/pagehelp/Content/Missions_Instruments/Lunar%20Reconnaissance%20Orbiter%20%28LRO%29/LOLA/GDR/GDRDEM.htm)). Older float files mix kilometres and metres. The app's loader already handles three such conventions.

**Grid geometry.** The pole sits at the corner of the four central pixels, not at a pixel centre. Polar stereographic scale is exact only at the pole: it is 1.0077 at 80°S and 1.0718 at 60°S, so a "240 m" cell at 60°S covers about 224 m of ground *[team calculation]*. Compute distances on the sphere. Check each file's orientation against a known grid, as `tools/build_hires_terrain.mjs` already does. The PGDA 104 files use "Moon (2015) South Polar Stereographic" ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/17954508)); confirm the projection definition before mosaicking them with PGDA 90.

**Product names.** Renames hide changes: LDEM_85S_10M became LDEM_83S_10MPP_ADJ and its latitude limit moved from 85° to 83°S ([Barker et al. 2023 preprint](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf)).

**File sizes.** The files are multi-gigabyte, so never fetch them in the browser. Whether PGDA sends cross-origin (CORS) headers is unknown. Ship precomputed horizon profiles instead; a 360° profile is a few kilobytes.

**Licence.** No licence statement was found on the PGDA pages. Before relying on the app's "Public domain (NASA)" label, find a primary statement.

**Sites near the 80°S edge.** Sites such as IM-1 and Cabeus B have horizons that run past the edge of the 20 m product. Fill them from the 60°S _ADJ grids, not from the 1 km grid. Nine of the app's 20 m tiles already differ by **9–24 m** from that 1 km grid *[app record]*.

**Tuning toward PGDA.** Do not adjust the model until it matches PGDA's ridge numbers, because those numbers move by up to 20 points with PGDA's own resolution.

## A browser route planner works as a strategic sketch

### Published planners map time and shadow, not just slope

NASA's VIPER team computed illumination maps **every 2 hours (4,128 maps)** and Deep Space Network visibility maps on a similar cadence, from 1 m terrain plus SPICE ephemerides ([VIPER LPSC 2023](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2023/pdf/2377.pdf); [Beyer et al. 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ae061a)). Planning was split into AI-assisted strategic routes and short tactical routes ([Fong 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240010930/downloads/viper-tech-2024-08-26.pdf)). Routes were built from legs of one lunar day, each starting at the landing site or at a "safe haven" ([Ennico-Smith et al. 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004239/downloads/SSR2023-VIPER-Planning-Ennico.pdf?attachment=true)). The central lesson is that **shadows and radio shadows move at about the rover's average speed**, so timing matters as much as geometry ([VIPER mission-planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf)).

The research line runs from TEMPEST, which searched over x, y, time and battery energy on precomputed lighting snapshots ([Tompkins et al. 2002](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/pub_files/pub3/tompkins_paul_2002_1/tompkins_paul_2002_1.pdf)), to Otten's space-time searches for continuously lit routes ([Otten et al. 2015](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/app/uploads/2018/01/ICRA2015_Otten_3109.pdf)). Otten found that some of those routes need speeds as low as 0.1 cm/s ([Otten et al. 2017](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/2018/01/FSR_2017_Otten_66.pdf)). More recent work includes risk-aware mission-level planning ([Lamarre et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.08558)) and a 2026 illumination-constrained spatio-temporal A* ([Miao & Wei 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/2/310)). Speyerer et al. found a least-energy route on the connecting ridge, computed at 20 m (the app's resolution), that stays lit **94.4% of the lunar year** ([Speyerer et al. 2016](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/optimized-traverse-planning-for-future-polar-prospectors-based-on/)). A web-based lunar south-pole rover planning and constraint-checking tool was presented at LPSC 2024 ([LPSC 2024 #2579](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2024/pdf/2579.pdf)), and NASA Moon Trek has added traverse-planning tools ([Moon Trek, COSPAR 2022](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022cosp...44..305L/abstract)). A browser planner is therefore a recognised format.

### Constraints the planner can defend with sources

No source gives canonical cost weights; every planner tunes its own. The app should offer presets and sliders rather than claim one optimal weighting. Slope and shadow limits, however, are well sourced:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| LTV, remote | 20° up, down and across | 6–8 km per 24 h | ≥20 km per charge | not published | assumption needed |
| Walking EVA | 20° (15° for planning) | about 2 km/h | 0.5–2 km from lander | ≤2 h continuous shadow | keep lander in sight where possible |
| Accessibility studies | 25° | — | 14–26 km one way | staging areas with Sun and Earth | — |

The VIPER row comes from NASA's [VIPER lunar operations page](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/lunar-operations/), the [VIPER mission-planning overview](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/overview_of_mission_planning_for_the_viper_rover.pdf) and the [February 2025 VIPER briefing](https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/psd/lunar-science/documents/2025-02-13_VIPER_Updated_Technical_Briefing_Presentation-Shared.pdf). The LTV row comes from [Litaker et al., NASA-TM-20240001217](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/artemis/resources/LitakerEtAl_NASA-TM-20240001217_LTV%20Teleops%20Study.pdf). The EVA row comes from the [xEVA ConOps](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/eva-exp-0042_xeva_system_con_ops_rev_b_final_dtd_10192020_ref_doc.pdf), the [Artemis III EVA tenets](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230008183/downloads/Tenets%20of%20Lunar%20EVA%20for%20Artemis%20III_Public%20Release.pdf) and an [Artemis III workshop abstract](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/Artemis_III_2023/pdf/2009.pdf), and the accessibility row from [Cannon & Britt 2020](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020EA001291). Some of these numbers conflict across documents. VIPER's survivable darkness ranges from 40–80 h to 96 h depending on the document's date ([Andrews 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003840/downloads/2020-06-24,%20VIPER%20Mission%20Overview%20%28Andrews%29.pdf)). The often-quoted 15 km/h LTV top speed has no primary source. These are 2020–2025 concept values. A slope cost that follows them treats 0–15° as normal, penalises 15–20° heavily, allows 20–25° only for an "exceptional" robot setting, and blocks anything above 25° *[team inference from the cited limits]*. For communications, NASA's relay planning targets about **75% availability** and quotes 80–86% with one relay ([LCRNS 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/lcrns-esper-257-1.pdf)). No minimum Earth elevation angle for a usable link was found. The honest option is a "relay assumed" toggle that reports the hours of direct Earth view, rather than inventing relay coverage.

### Algorithms that fit, and what they cost

Grid A* or Dijkstra on a raster cost map is the workhorse of both mission planners and GIS tools. GRASS `r.walk` is a direct precedent for direction-dependent slope cost, with optional 16-neighbour moves ([GRASS r.walk](https://grass.osgeo.org/grass85/manuals/r.walk.html)). A 2026 lunar study treats slopes above a threshold as impassable. It also notes that a simple linear slope penalty under-estimates the cost of soft, flat ground compared with a terramechanics model ([Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/astronomy-and-space-sciences/articles/10.3389/fspas.2026.1764826/full)). Curiosity's onboard planner uses Field D*, and Perseverance ranks candidate paths and checks them with a rover-scale clearance evaluation ([Perseverance ENav](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/397929378_Enhanced_Autonomous_Navigation_on_the_Perseverance_Mars_Rover)). That rover-scale hazard checking is exactly what a 20 m planner cannot do. Measured costs in V8:

| Task | Size | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Static A*, 6 km route on 20 m grid | 500² to 2000² cells | 15–52 ms |
| Static search, flood whole grid | 2000² cells (40 km) | about 1 s |
| Time-dependent earliest-arrival search with waiting | 250² to 1000² cells | 13–31 ms |
| Time-expanded search (cell × time slice), soft penalties | 250² × 24 to 500² × 32 | 0.4–1.2 s, 48–256 MB |
| Horizons per corridor cell, 19 km reach | 300² cells (6 km), 32 / 64 / 128 azimuths | 3.1 / 9.5 / 34.9 s |
| Sun test from stored horizons | 300² cells × 336 hourly steps | 0.37 s |

*[Team benchmark; synthetic terrain; desktop, single thread]*

The search is cheap; horizon precompute is the bottleneck. The app's own per-point horizon routine takes about 0.13 s per site *[app record]*. Running it for every one of 90,000 corridor cells would take hours, so the corridor needs a lighter, layered approach. One rule simplifies the time-aware mode a great deal. If the rover may wait in place, a **time-dependent Dijkstra with one label per cell gives the exact earliest arrival** under a hard rule such as "only enter cells that are lit when you arrive, and wait only in lit cells". This holds because waiting can never make arrival later (a standard property of time-dependent shortest paths). The full time-expanded search is needed only for soft trade-offs, such as accepting an hour of shadow to save a kilometre. A cheaper substitute is to run three to five weight presets and show the routes side by side. Skip RRT* (random, and the route changes on every click), fast marching (cannot tell uphill from downhill), D* Lite (incremental re-planning gains nothing when a full plan takes 50 ms) and full Field D* (A* plus line-of-sight smoothing gives most of the benefit).

The data fit as well. The app's existing 20 m tiles are about **200 KB each compressed for a 16 km square**; 76 tiles total 15 MB *[observation from `public/data/hires`]*. A route box of 6–10 km plus a 2–3 km margin therefore needs one to four tiles. Those tiles exist only near listed sites and over the nine Artemis III regions, though. Elsewhere only the 240 m grid exists, and it smooths away the slopes that matter. **Restrict routing to 20 m coverage**, or label anything else as unusable for slopes.

For time slices, 2 hours matches VIPER's cadence. At the pole the Sun's azimuth moves about 15° per day, or about 1.25° per 2-hour step *[team calculation]*. A 6 km route at VIPER's 1 cm/s average takes about **167 hours, about seven days** *[team calculation]*. That is about 84 slices, which fits in three 32-bit words per cell, or about 1 MB per layer for 90,000 cells.

The research notes suggested reusing each site's existing far-field horizon for every corridor cell. Watch the parallax. A cell 3 km from the site sees terrain 20 km away shifted by about 8.6° in azimuth, terrain 50 km away by about 3.4°, and terrain 100 km away by about 1.7° *[team calculation]*. A safer design computes full horizons at a lattice of anchor points about every kilometre along the corridor. At 0.13 s each, 10–50 anchors take about 1.3–6.5 s on one thread. Each cell then combines the nearest anchor's far-field horizon with its own local 20 m horizon of 32–64 azimuths and a reach of at least about 10 km. The research found no source giving the azimuth count or reach needed at route scale. Calibrate both by comparing lit fraction against the full routine at about 50 random corridor cells.

### Honest limits the interface must state

**The terrain cannot see what stops rovers.** VIPER's limit is a 20 cm step ([VIPER 2025 deck](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250004148/downloads/viper-2025-04-24.pdf)). Pragyan found a 4 m crater only 3 m ahead, using its own cameras, and had to back out ([Tribune India / ISRO](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/india/chandrayaan-3-pragyan-rover-comes-across-big-crater-on-lunar-surface-retraces-path-isro-releases-fresh-pictures-539165)). Yutu-2's ground team built 2 cm DEMs from its navigation cameras at every waypoint ([Remote Sensing 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/4/624)).

**No rock map covers the pole.** The global hazard layers stop short of it: Diviner rock abundance at ±70° ([Bandfield et al. 2011](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2011JE003866)) and the 2025 global boulder map at ±60° ([Aussel et al. 2025](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025JE008981)). Only site inventories exist, such as Boazman et al.'s 86,896 boulders around Artemis sites 001 and 004 ([Boazman et al. 2023](https://boris.unibe.ch/179288/1/Boazman_2022_Planet._Sci._J._3_275.pdf)). Soil strength is a lesser worry: boulder tracks suggest polar regolith bears loads much like equatorial regolith ([Bickel & Kring 2020](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020Icar..34813850B/abstract)).

**Slopes at 20 m read low.** Slopes measured over 20 m understate the local maximum, and between laser tracks they are interpolated.

**Energy figures are rough.** No published watt-hours-per-kilometre-versus-slope curve exists for VIPER or the LTV, and the app's battery model is a constant-load estimate.

**Earth view is not a radio link.** It is geometry only.

**Real routes cannot be checked.** No comparison between a planned and an actual lunar traverse was found, and the benchmarks above used synthetic terrain on a desktop, not a phone.

**Small delays matter.** Because shadows move at about rover speed, a route that works on schedule can fail after a few hours' delay. Re-run each route with departure shifted by ±2–4 hours and report whether it still holds. Also replay the chosen route with the full horizon routine every 100–200 m at the planned times, and report hours dark, hours without Earth, longest dark stretch, maximum slope and total climb. A suitable label is: **"Strategic route on 20 m LOLA terrain. Not checked for rocks, steps or craters smaller than about 20–40 m."**

## Conclusion and prioritised recommendation

The two questions share one piece of infrastructure: a banded, crest-preserving horizon built on PGDA's adjusted grids. Fixing the mid-field first therefore pays twice, once for site accuracy and again for the route planner's corridor horizons. The app has also reached a point where its disagreement with NASA largely reflects NASA's own resolution choices. Further tuning against PGDA's ridge numbers will chase a moving target. The more credible next tests are image-based shadow checks like the existing LROC comparison, and an honest per-site confidence figure.
